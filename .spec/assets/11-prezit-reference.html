Motivation · the line of reasoning

How we got here

Root
A programme has one budget and two levers: collect more data, or build a better model. Which one pays?
→
Literature · wiki
Error falls as a power law in both data and parameters, so which lever wins is empirical, not general. The field already paid for guessing once — a generation of models built far larger than their budgets justified.
→
q1 · now
The comparison gets made constantly and controlled almost never. Each arm runs at whatever budget suited it — so "more data won" and "more budget won" predict the same result.

Siblings still open: how much of the data do we actually need? · does any of it hold on data from elsewhere? — both wait on this one.

The question

You have one budget left this quarter. You can spend it labelling twice as much data for the model you already have, or on the bigger, better model with the data you already have. Which cheque do you write?

  • Does doubling the data beat the better model?  — the headline, and the comparison the literature usually reports.
  • Does it still win once the budgets are level?  — doubling the data also roughly doubles the compute, so the headline comparison confounds the two.

Scope: architecture family, adaptation protocol and seed count fixed before any run. Every comparison executed twice — once at equal data, once at equal compute. Verifiable bars pre-registered on all three hypotheses.

Method · the data & the target

What goes in — and what we predict

Target — Δ top-1 accuracy on held-out data
Every arm is scored against the better model on the original data and reported as a delta in accuracy points, so positive means the extra data won. Each arm is trained with 3 seeds and evaluated on a held-out split of 50,000 examples; every interval in this deck is the 95% interval across those seeds.

How a verdict is reached
Each hypothesis carries pre-registered verifiable bars, written before any run. On close, each bar is ticked met / unmet / could-not-evaluate and the verdict is derived mechanically from the ticks — all met is supported, any unmet is partial or refuted. Result 01 · does it survive a level budget?

Real at each arm's own budget — reversed once they're matched

each arm at its own budget
matched on training compute
  • At its own budget the data arm wins — +3.3 points on the first task, replicated at +1.8, both intervals clear of zero.
  • Matched on compute it loses — −4.6. A 7.9-point swing from levelling the budget alone.
  • The harder task never gave it a win to lose — −1.3 before matching, −5.1 after.
  • Three seeds per arm, so an interval spanning zero is a null, not a small effect: h3's own-budget result is exactly that.

We measured a budget difference, not a data effect

Verdict · s1, approved

More data wins on paper and loses on a level budget

Not on a level budget. Doubling the data beats the better model when each arm runs at its own cost — and that is the comparison the literature usually reports.

  • What closes: the headline claim, as stated, is a budget artefact. Matched on compute the advantage reverses; on a harder task it is gone before matching.
  • What opens: if the win was budget, the real question is how little data we can get away with — and whether any of it transfers off this distribution.
  • What we would not have caught: h1 alone passed all three of its pre-registered bars. It took h2 to make the confound visible.
